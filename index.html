IRB Consideration — Do you need ethics approval?
Required reading before your project begins
What is IRB? The Institutional Review Board (IRB) is the university body that reviews research involving human subjects to ensure it is conducted ethically. You may be required to obtain IRB approval before collecting any data from students — even surveys.
Projects that research teaching and learning require IRB approval, even if many are deemed exempt. If you plan to collect, analyze, or publish findings from student data (surveys, grades, work samples, interviews), IRB review is required. You will need to complete CITI Training and receive certification as well. Starting data collection before approval can invalidate your findings entirely and put you and your students at risk.

Project Support Needs & Capacity
Think through what your project will require
Answer honestly — these questions help you identify gaps early, before they become mid-project blockers.
Media & Content Production Learn more ↗
Media production involves real costs and lead time. Budget for this early — production support, licensing stock footage or music, accessibility requirements (captions, audio descriptions) can all add up. Factor this into your timeline.
Data & Analytics Learn more ↗
If you need data analysis support, plan for this well in advance. Options include hiring a graduate student with quantitative skills, collaborating with a colleague who has that expertise, or consulting with BU's Institutional Research office. This should be factored into your budget and timeline from the start.
Technology Learn more ↗
Technology checklist:
  1. Accessibility — does it meet WCAG standards and work with screen readers?
  2. Privacy — does it comply with FERPA for student data?
  3. Security — does it meet BU's data security requirements?
  4. Cost — licenses, per-seat fees, renewal costs.
  5. Vendor contracts — some tools require a formal Statement of Work reviewed by BU's legal team, which takes time.
Do not assume a tool is approved for use simply because it is popular.
Student Workers
Hiring student workers involves administrative steps that take longer than most faculty expect. You will need to post positions, conduct interviews, complete HR onboarding paperwork, and set up payroll — plan for 4–6 weeks minimum before a student worker can begin paid work. If your project depends on student worker contributions early in the semester, begin this process before the semester starts. Also confirm with your department whether a stipend or hourly employment is more appropriate for your project.
Time & Availability
Be specific. A realistic picture of availability is essential for building a workable timeline. If team members are on 9-month contracts and the grant does not include summer stipends, project work may need to pause or slow significantly during summer months.

Project Report
Based on the Shipley Pilot Project Report Template

Build your report progressively throughout the project — don't wait until the end. Each section below maps directly to the Shipley report template. Your guide can help you draft any section.

Dissemination goals: Beyond the project report, consider whether your findings are appropriate for conference presentation or journal publication. SoTL venues like the International Society for the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning (ISSOTL) and discipline-specific teaching journals are good starting points. Ask your guide for suggestions relevant to your field.

Project Team and Roles

List all team members, their titles, and their roles within the project. Note any special contributions.

Project Goal and Objectives

Describe the need addressed, the target audience, and your theory of change — how does this intervention lead to improved learning?

Milestones & Outcomes Achieved

List each milestone and outcome achieved. For anything not yet achieved or changed from the original plan, briefly explain why.

Impact

What changed as a result of this project? Describe changes observed by the course team or reported by students. Reference your Impact Evaluation Framework data.

Challenges

What risks and challenges arose? How did you address them? What support would have been valuable?

Lessons Learned

What did you learn from challenges, stakeholders, and the process itself? What emerged as best practices?

Next Steps & Future Plans

If ongoing: what are the next milestones? If complete: how will the project's impact continue? Will it be scaled, adapted, or published?
